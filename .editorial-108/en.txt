# Write a Customer Reply with ChatGPT: Facts, Promises and the Next Update
A customer needs an answer before their meeting. Your technical team has not established the cause. You can still write a useful initial reply without turning the customer's deadline into a recovery promise.

This is a fictional training case. The email, internal notes and completed replies below were written for this guide; they are not a real customer conversation or a recorded AI output.

## Start with two different times
> The CSV export button has stopped producing a file. I need the data for an internal meeting at 17:00 today. Will it be fixed in time? Please tell me what is happening.

The customer's 17:00 deadline describes their need. Your team's 15:00 commitment describes when you will send an update. Neither establishes when service will be restored.

| Information type | What the team knows |
| Confirmed | The report has been received and passed to the technical team for investigation. |
| Unknown | Cause, affected users, recovery time and a working alternative. |
| Approved commitment | Send a status update by 15:00, even if there is no progress. |
| Information to request | Time of the failure, browser and version, and any error message. |
| Not approved | Refunds, compensation, manual data delivery or a recovery deadline. |

## Give the AI the reply materials, not permission to fill the gaps
Use an AI environment approved for this information. Removing a customer's name does not necessarily remove confidential details.

```text
Draft an initial customer reply using only the customer email and internal notes below.
Acknowledge that the data is needed by 17:00.
Say that the report has been passed to the technical team for investigation.
Do not promise recovery by 17:00: the recovery time is unknown.
Promise a status update by 15:00, whether or not there is progress.
Ask for the failure time, browser and version, and any error message.
Do not add causes, troubleshooting instructions, workarounds, refunds or other commitments.
Return a subject and email body. In a separate internal section, list each commitment and the note that authorizes it.

[Paste the customer email and the table above.]
```

The separate commitment check is more useful here than a generic request to make the reply polite. It makes a specific error visible: changing a communication deadline into a repair deadline.

## A reply that fits the notes
> Subject: Update on the CSV export issue
>
> Thank you for reporting this. I understand that you need the data for your 17:00 meeting.
>
> We have passed your report to the technical team for investigation. The cause and recovery time are not yet confirmed, so I cannot promise that the export will be restored before your meeting. I will send you a status update by 15:00, even if there is no further progress.
>
> Could you share when the issue occurred, your browser and version, and any error message shown? Please leave out personal information or business data that is not needed for the investigation.
>
> I am sorry for the disruption.

This is an edited example of an acceptable reply, not a promise that every AI will produce this wording. The authorized promise is an update, not a repair.

## Repair a specific mistake without rewriting everything
| Draft wording | Why it is wrong here | Correction |
| “High system load caused the problem.” | The cause is unknown. | Say the report has been passed for investigation. |
| “The issue will be fixed by 15:00.” | 15:00 is the update deadline. | “I will send a status update by 15:00.” |
| “Please reinstall the application.” | No such troubleshooting step has been approved. | Request the known investigation details instead. |

A useful follow-up instruction is: “Change only the recovery promise in paragraph two. The 15:00 commitment is to communicate, not to restore service. Keep the other facts unchanged.”

## At 15:00, no progress is still an update
If the team confirms that investigation is continuing but still cannot estimate recovery, the next message can be short:

> As promised, here is the current status. The investigation is continuing, but the cause and recovery time remain unconfirmed.

Add another contact time only after agreeing it internally. Do not ask the AI to invent a reassuring deadline. The reader should finish this workflow with a draft that can be checked against a short, explicit list of facts and promises.

# Compare Quotes with ChatGPT: Put Three Proposals on the Same Basis
A per-session price, an all-inclusive quote and a per-person fee cannot be compared by choosing the smallest number on the page. First calculate the same required service, then check its mandatory conditions.

The following training procurement example is fictional. Amounts are in Japanese yen, excluding tax, solely to demonstrate a calculation. They are not market prices, supplier recommendations or measured AI results.

## The decision is a 30-person course, not a headline price
The organization needs training for 30 people, completed by 20 October, within JPY 450,000. Thirty days of questions and answers after the course is mandatory.

| Quote | Information supplied |
| A | JPY 180,000 per session; capacity 10. Three identical sessions serve 30 people. All three can finish before the deadline. Thirty-day questions and answers are included. No additional fees. |
| B | JPY 420,000 for all 30 people. Delivery before the deadline and thirty-day questions and answers are confirmed. No additional fees. |
| C | JPY 9,000 per person plus JPY 60,000 setup. Supports 30 participants. Delivery dates and the duration of questions and answers are not stated. No other fees are listed. |

A is not a JPY 180,000 solution for this group. Equally, C's missing support information does not establish that C offers no support.

## Keep mandatory requirements out of a weighted average
A low price cannot compensate for a mandatory condition that is not met. Separate requirements from preferences before requesting a recommendation.

```text
Compare these three training quotes for an internal selection meeting.
Requirements: 30 participants; total no more than JPY 450,000 excluding tax; completion by 20 October; 30 days of questions and answers after training.
Use only the supplied quote information.
1. Calculate the total for 30 people and show each formula.
2. For each mandatory requirement, mark met, not met, or unconfirmed.
3. Do not treat missing information as “not offered” or zero points.
4. Separate candidates that can currently be assessed from those requiring clarification.
5. Draft the questions needed before a decision.
Do not select a supplier solely because its stated price is lowest.
[Paste the quotes.]
```

## The completed comparison
| Check | A | B | C |
| Calculation | 180,000 × 3 | 420,000 total | 9,000 × 30 + 60,000 |
| Total, JPY excluding tax | 540,000 | 420,000 | 330,000 |
| Within 450,000 | Not met | Met | Met |
| Complete by 20 October | Met | Met | Unconfirmed |
| Thirty-day questions and answers | Met | Met | Unconfirmed |
| Current decision status | Outside this budget | All requirements confirmed | Clarify date and support before deciding |

B is the only option whose mandatory conditions are all confirmed. That does not prove B is the best supplier in every respect, or that C is unsuitable. It tells the decision-maker what the evidence currently permits.

Check the multiplication yourself or in a spreadsheet. In a real comparison, attach the quotation name, date and section to every material row. A neat table is not a substitute for that traceability.

## Turn missing cells into a useful supplier email
> We are considering training for 30 participants. Can it be completed by 20 October? Can participants ask questions for 30 days after the course? If both are possible, please confirm whether they are included in the quoted JPY 330,000 total.

This asks about the two conditions blocking the decision. “Please send more information” may simply produce another brochure. If the reply introduces an additional fee, update the calculation as well as the support cell.

## Reconsider preferences without moving the goalposts
```text
Keep participant count, completion date and thirty-day question support mandatory.
If C confirms these conditions, compare the eligible options with total price as the first preference.
Do not treat current unknowns as confirmed facts.
State which new information changed the conclusion.
```

If the budget itself changes, record that separately. Otherwise a later “better recommendation” might merely be answering a different question. This approach works for subscriptions, equipment or services too, provided you normalize the actual billing unit and do not invent missing prices.

## Use Prompt Ready to prepare changing comparison conditions
The reusable part is not a verdict about A, B or C. It is the structure: participants, deadline, mandatory conditions, pricing unit and how to handle missing facts. Put those conditions into Prompt Ready, review the prepared request, then use it in your selected AI. Keep the supplier evidence with the final table; the application does not approve a purchase or verify a quote on your behalf.

# Turn Meeting Notes into Action Items Without Inventing Owners or Dates
A person's name in a meeting note does not necessarily make them the task owner. “Ask Tanaka” may be Sato's action. A reliable follow-up list distinguishes decisions, proposals, cancellations and unresolved assignments.

The meeting and names below are fictional teaching material. The completed table is an edited answer key, not a transcript-processing benchmark.

## Eight notes, four different kinds of information
Assume the meeting took place on 5 October 2026.

```text
M1: Agreed to use the latest price list in the proposal.
M2: Yamada will share the candidate-service list by 7 October.
M3: Can the demo environment be ready by Friday? Its availability needs checking first.
M4: Sato will ask Tanaka whether a legal consultation is possible. No deadline set.
M5: The customer plans an internal review next week; the date has not been obtained.
M6: The comparison-table update is not needed this time. The earlier request is withdrawn.
M7: No owner for replacing the price list was assigned in this meeting.
M8: Set the next meeting after the customer's reply.
```

Friday in M3 is a question, not an agreed due date. M5 describes the customer's plan, not a task assigned to your team. M6 reverses an earlier request. These distinctions matter more than making the minutes sound polished.

## Extract before composing an email
```text
Organize the notes into:
1. Agreed decisions
2. Actions: task, owner, person to consult, deadline, supporting note number
3. Items requiring confirmation
4. Withdrawn work
Do not infer ownership from a name alone.
Do not turn a question into a deadline or the customer's plan into our action.
Keep missing owners and dates unconfirmed.
Merge genuine duplicates but retain supporting note numbers.
If notes conflict, show the conflict rather than silently choosing one.
Meeting date: 5 October 2026.
[Paste M1–M8.]
```

## An answer that preserves what was actually agreed
The decision is to use the latest price list. Assigning the replacement is still unresolved.

| Action | Owner | Consult | Due | Evidence |
| Share the candidate-service list | Yamada | Not applicable | 7 October | M2 |
| Ask whether legal consultation is possible | Sato | Tanaka | Not set | M4 |
| Replace the proposal's price list | Unconfirmed | Unconfirmed | Unconfirmed | M1, M7 |

The confirmation list contains demo availability, demo owner and timing; price-list owner and timing; the customer's review date; and the next meeting date. Friday remains a proposed point to check, not a confirmed task deadline. The comparison-table update belongs under withdrawn work, supported by M6.

An “unconfirmed” cell is useful information at this stage. Filling every cell would make the table look more complete while making it less accurate.

## Correct the row, not the whole document
```text
M4 assigns the checking action to Sato. Tanaka is the person to consult.
Correct only the owner and consultation fields in that row.
Do not change the other tasks or deadlines.
```

Similarly, a draft saying “Prepare the demo by 9 October” should be corrected to a confirmation item, because M3 asks a question. The calendar can resolve which date Friday means, but it cannot create an agreement that the meeting never made.

## Add new agreements explicitly, then write the follow-up
Suppose a later check establishes that Takahashi will replace the price list by 8 October. This is new information, not something extracted from the original notes. Update the evidence record before writing:

> Yamada will share the service list by 7 October. Following confirmation after the meeting, Takahashi will replace the price list by 8 October. Sato will ask Tanaka about legal consultation; the deadline remains open. Demo readiness and the next meeting date still need confirmation. The earlier comparison-table update has been withdrawn.

Before forwarding externally, check whether internal names and deliberations should be included. An acceptable internal follow-up is not automatically an acceptable customer email.

# Summarize a Long Document for a Decision, Keeping Numbers and Footnotes
“Annual cost: JPY 360,000; launch in November” is a clear summary. It is also wrong for the proposal below because it drops setup costs and launch conditions. A decision summary must stay connected to its source.

This guide uses five short sections written as a fictional proposal. They concentrate problems that can occur in a long document; they are not excerpts from a tested thirty-page customer document.

## Read the proposal and its conditions together
```text
S1 — Outline
Proposed rollout of a work-management service to all offices on 1 November.
The internal adoption decision is wanted by 20 October.

S2 — Fees
Setup: JPY 120,000. Monthly subscription: JPY 30,000.
The first year includes twelve monthly payments. All amounts exclude tax.
These are the only mandatory first-year fees.

S3 — Users
This quote covers 25 people. Additional-user prices are not stated.

S4 — Operating conditions and footnote
Start with one office; dates for other offices will be discussed after the trial.
Starting on 1 November requires user accounts and permissions to be finalized by 23 October.

S5 — Implementation
The internal owner and training date are not yet decided.
Time savings have not been measured.
```

S1 and S4 disagree about the initial rollout. Do not improve the prose by silently removing that conflict.

## Build an evidence table first
```text
Prepare decision notes from this proposal.
First extract fees, dates and their conditions, users and rollout scope, unresolved items, and contradictions.
Give a supporting section number for each row.
Separate stated amounts from your calculations.
Do not invent missing conditions or resolve conflicting wording yourself.
Then produce a short version for a manager and an action-focused version for the implementation team.
[Paste S1–S5.]
```

| Decision item | What can be established | Source |
| First-year cost | JPY 480,000 = 120,000 + 30,000 × 12, excluding tax | S2 |
| Users | 25; price above that number is missing | S3 |
| Start | 1 November, conditional on accounts and permissions by 23 October | S4 |
| Rollout | “All offices” conflicts with “one office initially” | S1, S4 |
| Owner and training | Not decided | S5 |
| Time saving | Not measured | S5 |

The calculated JPY 480,000 should not be presented as a number directly quoted from S2. Keeping the formula lets another reader check it quickly.

## The manager version leads with the blocked decisions
> First-year mandatory cost is JPY 480,000 excluding tax. The proposed 1 November start depends on accounts and permissions being finalized by 23 October. The proposal conflicts on whether the initial rollout covers all offices or one office, so scope needs clarification before adoption. Extra-user prices and the internal owner are unresolved; time savings have not been measured.

This retains uncertainty without reproducing the proposal paragraph by paragraph. A manager can see what needs a decision or a question.

## The implementation version makes unassigned work visible
| Preparation | Timing | What remains unresolved |
| Finalize the user list | By 23 October, as a start condition | No owner stated |
| Finalize permissions | By 23 October, as a start condition | No responsible person stated |
| Clarify office scope | Before confirming rollout | Resolve the S1/S4 conflict with the proposer |
| Arrange training | Date not decided | Set participants and date |

Do not fill the empty owner cells with “IT” merely because that seems likely. The table distinguishes preparation implied by explicit start conditions from assignments the document never made.

## Check five things before using either version
Have you retained setup cost, the conditional start date, the rollout conflict, the missing additional-user price and the absence of measured savings? If one disappears, ask for that point to be restored with its source section. Do not respond only with “make the summary more accurate.”

For a genuinely long document, number sections or pages, extract them in a consistent format, and consolidate the extraction. Finally, return to the original for important numbers, obligations, exceptions and contradictions. Information lost in an intermediate summary cannot be recovered merely by summarizing it again. If a table cannot be read, leave it for inspection rather than guessing its contents.

## Prepare different requests for different readers
Prompt Ready prepares the instruction, not the document analysis itself. For a manager, include cost, conditions and unresolved decisions. For the implementation team, include preparation, dates and unassigned work. Read the request, then supply it with the authorized source material to your chosen AI. The two completed versions should have different emphasis but the same facts.

# Ask ChatGPT Questions First: Turn a Vague Task into a Usable Brief
You do not need a perfect prompt before asking for help. But an unlimited interview can consume the time you hoped to save. Ask questions that change the work, then define when to stop and draft.

The handover situation and dialogue below are fictional examples, not a recorded AI session or a policy recommendation for every organization.

## Preserve the facts you have already supplied
```text
I need a handover guide for one person taking over the inquiry desk next month.
The handover lasts two weeks. The new colleague has completed basic product training.
Reply procedures exist, but staff disagree about when an inquiry should go to a manager.
```

“Who is the guide for?” and “Have they had training?” have already been answered. The missing information is what decisions the guide must support and which rules are actually approved.

## Set a short, decision-focused interview
```text
Do not write the handover guide yet.
Ask the most important missing questions one at a time, at most three in total.
Ask about matters that materially change the work before asking about tone or layout.
Do not repeat information already supplied.
Briefly explain why each question is needed.
Offer choices where useful, while allowing a free-text answer.
If I answer “not decided,” do not convert a suggested default into a fact.
After my answers, separate confirmed facts, unresolved matters, and the draft that can be made now.
```

Three is a limit for this example, not a universal optimum. The aim is to avoid refining formatting while the substantive task remains unknown.

## Different answers lead to different documents
| Question | Answer in this example | Consequence |
| Will the guide be used before work, during a difficult inquiry, or for reporting afterwards? | During an inquiry, when deciding whether to escalate. | Build a short decision aid, not a long training course. |
| Which escalation rules are approved? | Personal-data requests and compensation questions go to a manager. The rest is not standardized. | Include those two rules; do not invent a complete policy. |
| Who reviews and approves additional rules? | The inquiry-desk lead, at next week's meeting. | Prepare a review draft with explicit open questions. |

If the first answer had been “before starting the role,” the same source material might instead support an induction checklist. That is why use comes before formatting.

## “Not decided” does not always mean “do nothing”
| Situation | Confirmed handling | Still to check |
| Personal-data request | Refer to a manager | Contact method and urgent route |
| Compensation question | Refer to a manager | What to do if the approver is unavailable |
| Other uncertain inquiries | Rules not yet standardized | Which scenarios the lead must define |

This is a draft of the fictional organization's stated rules, not a generally applicable escalation policy. The unanswered questions are part of its usefulness: they show precisely what the lead must decide.

```text
Use confirmed facts as facts.
Leave matters requiring approval marked as awaiting confirmation.
You may propose headings and order, but label them as editorial suggestions.
Do not propose an approved compensation limit or other business rule unless it is in the supplied material.
```

## Redirect an unhelpful interview
If the AI asks about headcount again, reply: “The original brief confirms one new colleague. Move that to confirmed facts and continue with the remaining question.” If it asks about tone too early, say: “Tone can wait. First clarify the decision the guide must support.”

The stopping condition is not that every conceivable question has been answered. It is that you can distinguish the document you can draft now from the decisions someone else still owns.

## Use Prompt Ready to state the uncertainty clearly
Include the task, known background and intended output in Prompt Ready. When the material is incomplete, add “ask up to three important questions first” and “keep unapproved rules unresolved.” Review the generated request before copying it to your usual AI. Prompt Ready helps frame the request; neither it nor the AI should become the authority for the organization's missing rules.

# Practise a Sales Conversation with ChatGPT When the Customer Is Not Looking to Change
“Be a difficult customer” often creates an argument rather than a useful rehearsal. In an early sales conversation, practise finding out whether there is a reason to change before rushing to a demonstration.

The organization, product and exchanges below are fictional training material. They are not customer research, a real AI conversation, or evidence of improved sales results.

## Choose the information you need to discover
The customer is a sales-operations lead in a company with 40 salespeople. Departments submit spreadsheets for a monthly report. The lead consolidates them and has no current plan to replace the process. Extra work for the team is a concern.

Your objective is to understand the workflow, the conditions under which it becomes difficult, and what could be checked next. Getting a demo booked at any cost is not the objective.

```text
Play the customer in an early discovery conversation.
You are the sales-operations lead of a company with 40 salespeople.
You collect departmental spreadsheets for monthly reporting. You have no current plan to change tools and are concerned about rollout workload.
Respond naturally as the customer, one issue at a time.
Do not reveal every problem, budget and decision-maker before I ask.
Answer relevant questions. Do not disagree merely to make the exercise hard.
If I assert an unsupported feature or result, ask me to substantiate it.
Do not coach, score or give model answers during the roleplay.
When I say “finish,” stop playing the customer.
Begin: “Our current process isn't causing any major problems.”
This is fictional; do not infer facts about a real company.
```

## A pitch can hide the question you needed to ask
> Customer: Our current process isn't causing any major problems.
> Seller: Our service makes reporting more efficient. Let me show you a demo.
> Customer: We already produce the report. I don't want the team to learn another tool.

Nothing here establishes how reporting works or what the effect of a change would be. Treating this as an objection to overcome skips the discovery work.

## Four exchanges from the same opening
| Step | Seller | Customer |
| Understand the process | How does the monthly report get completed today? | Departments send their sheets; I align the fields and consolidate them. |
| Find the difficult condition | When does that process become harder to finish as planned? | When different departments define the numbers differently, there are extra questions back and forth. |
| Understand the impact | Which part of your work is affected by that back-and-forth? | The report is still on time, but there is less time to analyze it before the meeting. |
| Agree a useful next check | Could we review a shareable example of the fields and definitions before discussing a tool change? | I cannot share live data, but a sample with field names is possible. |

The result is not a proved need for your product. It is a clearer issue: inconsistent definitions reduce analysis time. The next conversation can examine definitions and workload instead of presenting every feature.

## Use actual statements as feedback evidence
```text
Finish the roleplay.
Review my statements against these questions:
1. Did I learn the current workflow?
2. Did I distinguish difficult conditions from their business impact?
3. Did I avoid claiming a solution without evidence?
4. Did I make the next check concrete?
For each, mark established or not yet established and cite a short statement from my side.
Suggest one improved question. Wait for my response before starting another round.
```

For the example above, the workflow and impact have been explored. The frequency of the problem and who approves shared definitions remain unknown. A useful next question is: “Who would need to agree if the departments used a common definition?”

## Change one condition, not the entire scenario
Repeat with the same workflow but add “the team cannot change its process this quarter.” Practise agreeing a small investigation without treating immediate rollout as inevitable. Compare the questions you asked, not an AI-generated likelihood of winning the deal.

# Ten Ways to Use ChatGPT at Work: Choose a Task and a Finished Result
Email and summarization are useful starting points. To broaden your use of AI, begin with a real piece of work: what material do you have, what result do you need, and how will you know it is ready?

This guide is a route into the task-specific articles, not a collection of supposedly universal prompts. You can use the examples without installing an application.

## Choose the job before choosing the wording
Writing a reply is creating a document. Extracting an owner from notes is organizing evidence. Comparing quotes is checking conditions. Rehearsing a sales conversation is practising a skill. These jobs need different materials and different checks.

| Job | Bring this material | Aim for this result | Check before using it |
| Reply to a customer | Received message, confirmed facts, next action | A reply draft | No invented cause or promise |
| Decline a request | Request, firm limits, approved alternatives | Clear refusal and next step | Start date is not mistaken for completion |
| Organize meeting notes | Notes of statements and decisions | Actions with owners and dates | Questions are not turned into agreements |
| Summarize a document | Source, reader and decision | Evidence-linked decision note | Numbers and footnotes are retained |
| Compare options | Quotes and mandatory conditions | A like-for-like table | Unknown does not mean zero or absent |
| Clarify a vague brief | What is known and what is difficult | A few useful questions | Already answered questions are not repeated |
| Rehearse a conversation | Role, stage and objective | Dialogue followed by feedback | Roleplay and coaching stay separate |
| Explain to a new audience | Accurate original and reader's knowledge | An accessible explanation | Simpler wording has not changed conditions |
| Draft instructions | Actual steps, exceptions and checks | A reproducible first draft | No imaginary buttons or steps |
| Organize research | Sources, dates and a question | Facts separated from interpretation | Sources support the individual claims |

Choose one row. You do not need to put every possible safeguard and output type into one enormous prompt.

## A small change in purpose changes the output
Suppose you have a proposal. “Summarize this” is not the same job as “identify what a manager must decide before adoption.” The latter may need a cost calculation, a rollout contradiction and missing responsibilities rather than a page-by-page recap.

Or suppose you have three quotes. Start by normalizing participant count and billing units. Ask for a recommendation only after distinguishing mandatory requirements from preferences. This makes the request useful even when the result is “clarify these two points first.”

Purpose: [what the reader will decide or do]
Source material: [the message, notes, document or data]
Confirmed conditions: [dates, limits, required facts]
Unknowns: [what must not be guessed]
Output: [reply, table, questions, checklist or dialogue]

These fields are prompts to think, not a mandatory form. A simple task may need only three of them. When a response goes wrong, check which decision-making information was absent rather than simply asking for more detail.

# Turn a Photo or PDF into a Three-Question Recall Sheet on iPhone
A page can be readable without being a good quiz. Make one useful recall sheet by deciding exactly what the learner should answer and what context must remain visible.

The small worksheet below was written for this article. Its water-cycle explanations are based on USGS material; the questions and layout are our teaching example, not a screenshot of automatic app detection.

## Start with a source you can check
| Source | Term | Short explanation |
| R1 | Evaporation | Liquid water changes into water vapor. |
| R2 | Condensation | Water vapor changes into liquid water. Tiny droplets can form clouds. |
| R3 | Precipitation | Water falls from clouds as rain, snow or other forms of precipitation. |

The useful distinction is a change of state versus water falling. A learner who swaps condensation and precipitation needs to return to R2 and R3, not merely look at the right word once. See [USGS: condensation](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/condensation-and-water-cycle) and [USGS: precipitation](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/precipitation-and-water-cycle).

## Hide the answer, not the whole explanation
| Keep visible | Hide | Why |
| “Liquid water changes into water vapor” | Evaporation | The state change is the clue being understood. |
| “Water vapor changes into liquid water” | Condensation | This distinguishes the reverse process. |
| “Water falls from clouds as rain or snow” | Precipitation | Falling, not just forming droplets, is central. |

Hiding both the term and its explanation leaves no meaningful question. Leaving the same term in a nearby heading gives the answer away. Inspect the whole page, including captions, before deciding that a mask is sufficient.

When most of your work happens in one window, repeatedly reopening a document can interrupt the task. A small reference view can keep the relevant part visible without giving half the display to a document you only consult.

Use the same task to choose between window layouts. The following specification table and decision memo are fictional teaching material, not a timed productivity test.

## A reference table and a memo to complete
You need a component delivered within two weeks, with at least a one-year warranty. The example prices are arbitrary units, not real offers.

| Option | Lead time | Example price | Warranty |
| A | 2 weeks | 120 | 1 year |
| B | 3 weeks | 100 | 2 years |
| C | Unconfirmed | 90 | Unconfirmed |

Your memo should say that A meets the stated timing and warranty conditions, B misses the timing condition, and C requires clarification. It should not select C merely because 90 is the smallest number. Keep the condition labels visible while writing; copying only the price column removes the context that makes the comparison meaningful.

## Choose a layout for how often you operate each window
| Work pattern | Useful starting layout | Reason |
| You edit both the source and memo repeatedly | Standard side-by-side windows | Both interfaces need room for interaction. |
| You write in the memo and occasionally consult a few source rows | Main window plus a small reference view | The writing area remains the primary workspace. |
| You need a whole-page diagram or dense table | A larger source window or side-by-side view | Small text or missing context would defeat the purpose. |

macOS provides window tiling and Split View for working with multiple windows. A reference utility is an additional option, not a reason to abandon those built-in layouts.

## Make the reference small enough to help, not too small to read
Open the source document and the memo. In Pocket Screen, choose the source window for a reference view, keep the relevant rows and their headers visible, then return to writing in the memo. Adjust placement so the view does not cover the line or controls you are editing. If reading requires repeated enlargement, give the source more space instead of forcing it into a tiny corner.

The key value is retaining the part you need while the main application stays usable. This is different from treating every source window as equally important or showing an entire desktop inside a miniature preview.

## Distinguish a live reference from a saved state
A live reference is appropriate when you need the source's current state. A frozen reference is useful when comparing a state that may change. After freezing, do not assume later changes in the source are reflected there. Label or otherwise remember when the state was captured.

Do not infer independent, simultaneous capture of all browser tabs from a demonstration of one browser window. Changing what that window displays and retaining a state is a different operation. Check the currently released product's available controls rather than assuming a future demonstration is already shipping.

# Make a Screen-Sharing Demo Easier to Follow: A 30-Second Script
A moving pointer does not tell the audience why a control matters. Coordinate the sentence, the target, the operation and the visible result so the viewer does not have to guess which change to follow.

Below is a fictional interface exercise, not a measured usability test. Both “Immediate notifications” and “Weekly summary” start on. The goal is to turn off immediate notifications while leaving the weekly summary on.

## Write what the audience should learn first
The outcome is not “I clicked a switch.” It is “Immediate notifications are off; the weekly summary remains on.” Naming the result stops the presenter from showing several unrelated settings just because they are nearby.

| Time | Say | Do | Attention cue |
| 0–5 seconds | “We will stop immediate notifications but keep the weekly summary.” | Leave both controls visible | No extra highlight yet |
| 5–10 seconds | “This is the immediate-notifications control.” | Position the pointer at that label/control | Brief pointer highlight |
| 10–16 seconds | “These two settings are separate.” | Show the two-row area without changing either | A short range highlight if helpful |
| 16–22 seconds | “I am turning only this one off.” | Click the immediate-notifications switch once | Keep the action visible; avoid unnecessary movement |
| 22–30 seconds | “Immediate is now off; weekly is still on.” | Pause on the final state | Let the result, not another animation, be the focus |

The timings are an editorial rehearsal target. They are not a measured optimum or an app performance claim.

## Use a point for a point, and a range for a relationship
A pointer highlight can answer “which button?” A range can answer “which rows belong to this explanation?” Neither replaces readable labels, suitable zoom or a sentence explaining the purpose. Highlighting the whole screen when only one switch matters can make the target less clear.

Pointer Cue can provide temporary pointer and range emphasis on Mac. Use the gesture configured in the installed release. Do not add persistent drawings when the explanation only needs a momentary marker; equally, choose a drawing tool when an arrow or annotation must remain for later reference.

## Rehearse without assuming the audience saw the click
After the operation, state the resulting status and hold it briefly. Saying “as you can see” is less informative than naming the two final states. A viewer joining late or using a small screen can then reconnect the action with its purpose.

Run the same script once without extra cues and once with a pointer/range cue. Compare whether the target and final state can be identified, not how many visual effects appear. This exercise helps you choose where a cue is useful; it is not evidence that one version is universally better.

## Check the receiving view, not only your own monitor
Before a real remote demonstration, use harmless content and check the participant's view at the intended resolution and sharing mode. Cursor size, small text and subtle lines may not remain equally clear after capture and transmission. The supplied product demonstration illustrates local actions, not a receiver-side compression test.

## Keep the software demonstration distinct from this worksheet
A product clip showing a website can explain how temporary highlighting looks. It is not the execution record for the fictional notification settings above. Likewise, a pre-release clip should not be used to promise features or entitlement rules in the current release.

## Turn the script into your next demo
Replace the two fictional settings with one real, non-sensitive task. Write its before and after states, select one point where a marker adds information, and practise the pause after the change. Use Pointer Cue where a temporary cue supports the sentence. Leave it quiet where the interface result already makes the meaning clear.
